Backed, or Just Named

Thirteen contracts on Base forged five of Coinbase's tokenized-equity tickers and traded $393,805 before they stopped. The genuine tokens sitting beside them are backed one-for-one. Neither the name nor the bytecode tells the two apart, and one on-chain check does.

The Challenge

Writing in Bloomberg on 9 September 2026, Matt Levine set out a useful taxonomy for tokenized stocks. A token carrying a company's name, he observes, can be one of exactly four things. It can be stock, issued on-chain by the company itself. It can be an SPV: "I buy 1,000 AMC shares, I put them in a pot, and I issue exactly 1,000 tokens against that pot." It can be a swap, an IOU for the asset's return. Or it can be nothing: "I issue 1,000 tokens each representing one AMC share, and you buy them, and I don't owe you anything. They're just, like, crypto tokens with the name ‘AMC’ on them." He notes that the fourth is out of fashion in equity tokenization, "though it is a very traditional approach in crypto generally."

To be clear about what is borrowed here and what is not: Levine is writing about Robinhood, whose tokens Robinhood says are "backed 1:1 by the corresponding underlying equity." On his own taxonomy those are the second kind. He is not describing the contracts in this case study and has never seen them. The ladder is his; the objects below are not.

What the taxonomy leaves open is the question a buyer actually has. Four categories are named, but nothing in that piece says how someone holding a token would work out which of the four they are holding. That question has an on-chain answer, and it is narrower and more useful than it first appears.

What measurement can and cannot settle

A rung is a claim about the world off the chain. Whether shares are sitting in a custodian's account is not visible from a contract, so on-chain data cannot confirm the first three rungs. No amount of reading state proves a pot exists.

It can do the opposite. On-chain data can show that nothing is behind a token at all, which rules out the first three and leaves only the fourth. That asymmetry is the whole practical value: the check is cheap, it runs in one direction, and almost nobody runs it. What follows is a namespace where running it separates the second rung from the fourth inside a single set of search results.

What We Found

Coinbase's B20 tokenized equities trade on Base. Four are live: NVDAc, AAPLc, METAc and GOOGLc, tracking NVIDIA, Apple, Meta and Alphabet. Since 1 July 2026 they have traded $241,972,775, which is 0.167% of all Base DEX volume in the same window, and Aerodrome Slipstream carries 86.46% of it.

Alongside them, in the same window, thirteen impostor contracts forged five of those tickers, adding COINc to the four above. Together they traded $393,804.68. Ten of the thirteen ran a uniform pattern: roughly half an hour of life, between 58 and 71 distinct takers, then nothing. The three COINc contracts break the pattern, each drawing a single taker, two of them accounting for $138,882 between them in consecutive half-hours.

The address shape is not an identifier

Coinbase's B20 contracts share a vanity address prefix, 0xb200000000000000000000, and Base's own documentation presents that prefix as the convention for the standard. It reads like a namespace badge. It is not one.

The impostors copy the prefix precisely so that a prefix check clears them. A first pass at measuring this asset class matched on that prefix and returned 7,532 distinct tokens on Base, with forged NVDAc, AAPLc and METAc rows sitting at the top of their tickers. Anyone reading the address shape as proof of provenance gets the wrong answer, and gets it confidently.

The name does not separate them either

The obvious fallback is to read name() and check it against the company. That fails, and it fails in the most expensive possible way: it clears the best forgeries and flags only the lazy ones.

Two of the thirteen return a name() of NVIDIA Corporation, byte-identical to Coinbase's own contract. Four use Meta Platform Inc., one character away from the real Meta Platforms Inc. Four more simply return the bare ticker. A name check waves through the two most convincing fakes and catches the four that were never going to fool anyone.

And neither does the bytecode

Bytecode fingerprinting is the technique that cracked the mainnet impersonator fleet, where 1,005 contracts shared one byte-identical 5,150-byte runtime. It returns nothing here. eth_getCode at every address in this namespace, genuine and forged alike, is the single byte 0xef. There is no runtime to hash and no fingerprint to compare.

How We Detect It

One check survives, and it is the one that reads the backing rather than the branding. Coinbase's contracts carry 8 decimals and a supply denominated in shares. Every impostor carries 18 decimals and exactly 1,000,000,000 units. 

-- Base mainnet, read live 2026-09-09
-- canonical NVDAc
decimals()    = 8
totalSupply() = 15,473.08          -- a share count

-- forged NVDAc, same vanity prefix, same name() string
decimals()    = 18
totalSupply() = 1,000,000,000.00   -- a round number, chosen not derived

The reason this works is worth stating carefully, because the intuition runs backwards. A supply of one billion at 18 decimals is not a forgery signature. It is the ordinary default in this namespace: five unrelated tokens sampled on the same prefix are all 18 decimals and four of the five carry exactly one billion. The impostors do not look wrong. Coinbase's tokens look unusual, because 8 decimals and a share count are what one-for-one backing forces you into. The test only works if you already know what the genuine article looks like, which is another way of saying the issuer's published contract list is doing the real work.

The stronger signal: the supply moves

A static reading is a snapshot, and a forger could copy a share-shaped number. What is much harder to fake is the behaviour of that number over time. A claim backed one-for-one has to be created when someone deposits and destroyed when someone redeems, so its supply breathes. Reading the same four contracts six days apart:

Token Supply 2026-09-03 Supply 2026-09-09 Movement
NVDAc (canonical)12,640.0815,473.08created

METAc is the useful row. Its supply went down, which is what redemption looks like from outside and is not a thing a fixed-supply token can imitate without someone actually burning tokens. A share count that moves in both directions is the on-chain shadow of a pot that is being added to and drawn down. A round billion that never moves is the shadow of nothing.

This still does not prove the shares exist. It is consistent with the second rung and inconsistent with the fourth, which is the most that reading a chain can honestly deliver.

What This Does Not Show

It does not verify Coinbase's custody. Everything above is consistent with one-for-one backing and rules out the "nothing" case. Confirming that shares sit in an account is an audit question, not an on-chain one, and no measurement in this study touches it.

The forged volume is small and no victim flow was traced. $393,805 against a $144.7 billion chain window is immaterial, and it is not claimed that anyone was harmed. What the takers believed they were buying was not measured. The finding is that the forgeries were indistinguishable by the checks most people would run, not that they succeeded at anything in particular.

The window is 1 July to 3 September 2026, and volumes are a leg sum. A leg sum counts both sides of a trade and is an upper bound rather than the collapsed per-transaction figure used elsewhere in this studio's reporting. The thirteen impostors are the ones that appeared in that window; nothing rules out others before or since.

Supply figures drift, which is the point of the section that uses them. The 3 September readings were correct when taken and had all moved by 9 September. Any figure here is a timestamped reading and not a standing fact.

The separate Ethereum mainnet fleet is a different operation. The 1,005-contract fleet in The Liquidity Mirage shares a playbook with these contracts, not an operator. No link between them was established or looked for.

Contract identity here was read directly from Base via eth_call and eth_getCode on 2026-09-03 and again on 2026-09-09, rather than taken from an indexer's symbol column. Volumes come from a single query execution against decoded DEX trades on 2026-09-03.

What It Proves to a Client

Every intuitive check on this namespace fails. The address prefix is shared by 7,532 tokens. The name is copyable and was copied byte-for-byte. The bytecode is a single opcode for everyone. Three plausible verification strategies, all of them returning a confident wrong answer, in an asset class explicitly built to carry the credibility of regulated securities.

The check that works is the one that asks what is actually behind the token and then watches whether that number behaves like a claim on something. That is the same discipline as the rest of the work in this studio, whether the subject is fabricated liquidity, execution quality or attribution: read the underlying state, key on identifiers rather than labels, and prefer "I don't know" to a confident wrong answer.

Levine's fourth rung is the cheapest thing to manufacture in crypto and, for the moment, the easiest to mistake for the second. Independent verification gets more valuable in exact proportion.

Where Dollar Stablecoins Sit Against $1

The OCC is encouraging community banks to issue payment stablecoins. Before any do, I measured where 45 fiat-backed dollar coins sat against $1 over 30 days, and how much of that distance moved with USDT.

Andrew Maury
• October 2026

On 21 October 2026 the OCC finalized its rule under the GENIUS Act, and its community-bank fact sheet says the agency is "encouraging the development of payment stablecoin capabilities within the community banking sector." The OCC is not taking applications yet, and the rule takes effect on 18 January 2027.

A bank deciding whether to issue a public coin can already look at the coins that exist. So I measured where today's fiat-backed dollar stablecoins sat against $1 over the last 30 days, one number per coin. The page describes where they sat and makes no claim about why.

What I measured

DefiLlama publishes the two things this needs: a list of stablecoins with each one's supply and how it is backed, and a daily price for each. I took both on 27 October 2026. From the list I kept every coin pegged to the US dollar, labelled fiat-backed, with more than $1 million outstanding, which is 73 coins. For each one I took the distance of every daily price from $1, in basis points, over the 30 days from 28 September to 27 October, and used the median.

Two rules decide which coins count. A daily price of exactly 1.0 is dropped as a fixed value, because at least one product reports a constant: BlackRock's BUIDL fund reads exactly 1.0 every day. None of the coins that end up counted had a reading of exactly 1.0 in the window, so the rule removed no data from them. And a coin counts only if it has a usable price on at least 20 of the 30 days. 28 coins fail that test, 27 of them under $1 billion, and BUIDL is the 28th. The 45 that pass have between 28 and 30 days each.

The fiat-backed label is DefiLlama's, and it is broader than the payment stablecoins the rule covers. It includes some yield-bearing tokens and at least one SEC-registered security, Figure's YLDS. I left them in, and the table under the chart lists every counted coin, so anyone can drop the ones they would not count.

What the numbers show

  • The large coins sat close. Seven coins have more than $1 billion outstanding. Five sat within 2.2 bps of $1: USDG and RLUSD at 1.0, USDGO at 1.3, PYUSD at 2.0 and USDC at 2.2. USD1 sat at 7.0 and USDT at 7.6.
  • The smaller coins spread out. The other 38, between $1 million and $1 billion, sat a median 10.3 bps from $1. Three of them were under 5 bps, and four were more than 40 bps away.
  • Size mattered across the whole set, and much less below $1 billion. Across all 45, the rank correlation between supply and distance is −0.42: larger coins tended to sit closer. Among the 38 coins under $1 billion it is −0.16, too weak to call a relation.
  • One issuer's coins can sit far apart. Paxos Trust Company issues both PYUSD, with $2.9 billion outstanding, which sat at 2.0 bps, and USDP, with $22.5 million, which sat at 21.7. The four coins in the set that Anchorage Digital Bank issues (USDGO, USDPT, USDtb and USAT) sat at 1.3, 3.1, 7.2 and 10.4. Sources for each issuer are on Anchorage's reserves page and, for USDPT, in The Block.
  • The bank-issued coin sat in the middle. SoFiUSD, issued by SoFi Bank, an insured national bank, sat at 6.8 bps, with a worst single day of 19.6.

How much of the distance is USDT's

USDT moved partway through the window. Its signed median was −2.0 bps over the ten days from 28 September to 7 October and −8.3 bps from 8 October on, so its discount roughly quadrupled. Most of the field moved the same way: 33 of the 45 coins had a lower signed median after 8 October than before, and 35 of the 45 sat farther from $1.

If a coin's price is derived partly from its trading against USDT, a move in USDT shows up in that coin's distance from $1. DefiLlama does not document how each price is built, so I cannot say which coins that applies to. What I can measure is the other reference: against USDT's price on the same day, the 38 smaller coins' median distance is 8.0 bps, against 10.3 from $1. Part of what reads as distance from par may be USDT's, and this data cannot separate the two.

Over 90 days

The same method over 90 days gives smaller numbers. Taking the coins with enough prices for that window, again seven above $1 billion and 38 below though not quite the same 38, the large coins' median is 1.6 bps and the smaller coins' 7.5, or 5.8 against USDT. Twelve of the smaller coins sit under 5 bps, against three over 30 days. The 30-day window carries the October move in USDT, so it is the less flattering of the two. The chart shows it because it is the current one.

What the rule says that bears on this

Three points from the final rule and the statute behind it.

  • An insured bank issues through a subsidiary. Under the GENIUS Act, an insured bank must issue a payment stablecoin through a subsidiary (12 U.S.C. 5901(23)).
  • A tokenized deposit is a different instrument. In the words of the OCC's preamble, the Act's definition of a payment stablecoin "explicitly excludes deposits, regardless of whether the deposit is recorded using distributed ledger technology." A deposit can pay interest. A payment stablecoin's issuer cannot pay holders interest "solely in connection with the holding, use, or retention" of the coin (12 U.S.C. 5903(a)(11)).
  • Secondary trading and supervision. The OCC says the rule's redemption requirements "do not apply to secondary market trading," and also that it "intends to review" secondary-market information "for supervisory purposes."

What this does not show

  • Trades. These are price-feed values, one a day. They say nothing about how much could be bought or sold at that price.
  • Redemption. What a coin trades at and what its issuer pays on redemption are separate questions. Nothing here measures the second.
  • Causes. Size, issuer, chain, trading venue and how the price feed is built all vary at once across 45 coins, and I have not separated them.
  • More than one window. The 30-day picture includes a move in USDT that changes it, as the 90-day figures show.

What I would track from launch

For a bank that issues a public coin, the inexpensive version of this is a few figures recorded every day from the first one: the coin's worst daily distance from par, the same against USDT, and which trading pairs its price feed is built from, since that decides how much of a USDT move lands in the coin's own number.

Data

DefiLlama's public stablecoins and stablecoinprices endpoints, both taken on 27 October 2026. Every figure on this page comes from one script run against that snapshot, which also draws the chart. The snapshot and the script are available on request.

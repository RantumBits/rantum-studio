The Metric That Moved 82 Percent and Measured Nothing

The largest move in ClearTrace's published execution data last quarter came mostly from the calendar and the population. What survives is half as large and has no attributable cause.

Andrew Maury
• October 2026

ClearTrace publishes a median execution slippage for every venue it measures. On 21 October the cell for ParaSwap on Ethereum read 6.40 basis points, over 17,913 trades in the trailing seven days. On 29 July the same cell read 36.83. That is a fall of 82.6 percent, and it is the largest move over the quarter of any venue cell ClearTrace publishes.

Executing through ParaSwap did not get 82 percent cheaper. Most of that figure comes from where the 90-day window happened to fall and from a change in who was trading. The part that survives is about half as large, and the cell has no way to say what caused it. Each of the reasons applies to any metric reported as the change between two dates, which is why they are worth going through one at a time.

What the number is

The cell is a median. For every fill routed through the venue on Ethereum in a trailing seven-day window, ClearTrace values both legs at that minute's oracle price, takes the size of the gap between them, and reports the median in basis points. It is computed on Dune from dex.trades, where the venue is labelled velora, the name ParaSwap now trades under. Each sync stores the reading and the number of trades behind it, and between 1 July and 21 October there are 52 of those snapshots.

The window opened on the series high

36.83 is the highest reading in the whole series, and it is the reading the 90-day window opens on. 6.40 is the lowest, and it is the reading the window closes on. A change measured between those two dates is the full range of the series, by the accident of where the calendar landed.

The opening value is also older than its date. It was first recorded on 24 July and appears again, identical, on 27 July and on 29 July. That is a table that was not refetched: 40 of the 42 venue cells carry the same trade count on all three snapshots. The window opens on a measurement taken five days before the window began.

Between the endpoints the series does not trend. It rose to 29.43 in late August, fell to 16.62 by mid-September, climbed to 30.92 by the end of September and dropped 17 points the following week. That pattern is ordinary in this data. Across 35 per-chain cells in the snapshot history, leaving out the ones ClearTrace has withdrawn or does not publish, the median net change over the quarter is 2.67 bps and the median range is 10.44. For the typical cell, the range inside the quarter is about three times the net move across it.

Then the population tripled

The last step down, from 8.89 on 14 October to 6.43 on 19 October, coincides with the count behind the reading going from 6,838 trades to 16,940. Two days later it was 17,913. Both readings cover seven days, so the week ending 19 October held about two and a half times as many ParaSwap trades on Ethereum as the week ending 14 October.

The jump belongs to that venue on that chain. The same venue on Base went from 4,961 trades to 5,003 on the same sync. Among the other 33 venue cells, the largest rise was 1.41 times, and 23 of them fell. Dune's public model repository does not explain it either: the ParaSwap models there were last changed in June.

The new flow did not arrive through the two ParaSwap entrypoints that the snapshot history tracks separately. Between them they held 1,487 trades before the jump and 1,396 after it. Roughly ten thousand trades a week came through other contracts, and identifying them takes a query I have not run for this piece. Without it, the most that can be said is that a median over 6,838 trades and a median over 16,940 trades are medians over different populations.

There is one more signal in the closing reading. On 21 October the signed median, which keeps the direction of each gap, was 0.67 bps, and 53.9 percent of scored fills were costs to the taker. The distribution was close to centred on zero. ClearTrace's own methodology note explains why that matters: when the gaps centre near zero, the absolute median measures how noisy the oracle is for those token pairs more than it measures execution. The window closes on a reading close to that regime.

The surge has since passed. Read from ClearTrace's public API on 27 October, the same cell shows 7.72 bps over 7,394 trades, with a signed median of 3.14 and 66.4 percent of fills as costs. The count is back near where it sat before 19 October, and the reading has moved back up with it.

A steady count is not a steady population

My first reading of this went wrong in a way worth recording. One of those two entrypoints, AugustusV6_2, fell from 62.31 bps to 4.31 over the same quarter while its count barely moved, from 871 trades to 862. I took that as the part of the fall that was real: same population, lower cost.

It does not hold. That row was refreshed far less often, with 11 distinct readings across 38 snapshots, and its window also opens on its own high and closes on its own low. Its readings span 4.31 to 62.31 on counts that never left the range from 627 to 1,239.

The venue cell shows the same thing more plainly. Between 29 September and 5 October it fell from 30.92 to 13.81 while its count moved by two trades, from 5,919 to 5,921. A count says how many fills went into a median and nothing about which ones. The mix can change completely under an unchanged count, and a median follows the mix.

What survives

Set the endpoints aside and compare whole months instead. The median of the distinct readings was 19.44 bps in August and 20.78 in September. In the first half of October, before the count jumped, the five readings ran from 13.81 down to 8.89 with a median of 11.36, and four of the five sit below every reading from August and September.

So something did move, on a count that held between 5,900 and 6,900 trades: from about 20 bps to about 11, a fall of roughly 45 percent. That is about half the headline figure, and it is the most the cell supports. Whether it reflects cheaper execution, a different mix of pairs and sizes, or a different set of integrators sending flow, a median cannot distinguish.

How the 82 percent got chosen

I found this number by asking which metric had moved most over the last 90 days, ranking every per-chain cell by the change between its first and last reading in the window, and taking the top.

That ranking leans toward exactly this case. Of the 41 per-chain cells with at least ten snapshots in the window, three open on their own series high and close on their own series low, or the reverse. The ranking put those three first, fourth and seventh. Asking for the biggest mover between two dates is partly asking which series the calendar caught at its extremes.

The rest of the top of the list makes the same point from another side. Three of the six largest moves sit in cells ClearTrace has withdrawn or does not publish, and two of those were withdrawn after we found the number did not mean what the column said. A large move is a reason to inspect a cell before it is a finding.

What would make the comparison mean something

  • Compare periods, with their spread. A month of readings has a median and a range. A change smaller than the range inside each month is weak evidence of anything.
  • Print the count beside every value. A reading whose count jumps far outside its usual range starts a new series, and it should be read as one rather than as the next point on the old line.
  • Mark carried-forward readings. A snapshot that repeats the last value because a table was not refetched is not a new observation, and a window that opens on one inherits its age.
  • Hold the population fixed where the question allows it. The comparison practitioners use when it has to hold up is implementation shortfall: the price quoted against the price achieved, on the same order, so every observation carries its own baseline. ClearTrace's quote sampler is built that way, pairing each solicited quote with a fill simulated against chain state at the same moment.

Why this one drew no questions

A cost metric that rises 82 percent gives the venue a reason to write in and ask why. One that falls 82 percent gives nobody a reason to do anything. Errors in the flattering direction last longer for that reason alone, because the feedback that catches the other kind never arrives.

ClearTrace has met this before. Its Scorecard Edition 3 records that the cell for the 1inch Limit Order Protocol published 13.27 bps where the population left after removing one resolver's maker-side edge measured 29.73. The venue read about twice as good as it was, and the number drew no complaint.

The moves that most need checking are the ones in the direction everyone likes. This one was a published number and the largest of the quarter, and close to half of it was the calendar and the population rather than the venue.
